Abstract
Emergent behavior in complex systems arises from local interactions and distributed feedback
without centralized control, producing rich global organization. While extensively studied
in natural systems and simulations, emergence in designed robotic systems remains diﬃcult
to intentionally shape, compare, and evaluate. In particular, how organizational structure,
physical embodiment, and human intervention jointly inﬂuence emergent swarm behavior is
still poorly understood.
This thesis investigates how organizational diﬀerentiation and feedback policies, embodied robotic constraints, and human-in-the-loop cybernetic control can be designed to modulate the complexity, adaptability, and legibility of emergent behaviors in robotic swarms.
Focusing on modular and reconﬁgurable robotic systems, the research frames emergence not
as a byproduct of control algorithms, but as a tunable behavioral outcome shaped by system
organization and interaction structure.
The work combines digital swarm simulations, physical prototyping of reconﬁgurable
modules, and human-interaction experiments to systematically explore how variations in
communication topology, role diﬀerentiation, and intervention modalities aﬀect emergent
patterns. A set of measurable behavioral metrics, including pattern diversity, adaptability,
robustness, and human interpretability, is proposed to evaluate emergent complexity across
conﬁgurations.
By establishing a comparative evaluation framework for emergence in robotic swarms,
this research contributes a design-oriented understanding of how complex behaviors can be
intentionally structured, intervened upon, and assessed in humanrobot collectives.

Introduction

1.1

Introduction

Emergent collective behaviorsuch as ﬂocking birds, ant colonies, or cellular tissuesoften arises
without centralized control. Instead, complex global patterns emerge from many agents
following simple local rules. This phenomenon, commonly referred to as emergence, has
been widely studied in ﬁelds such as biology, physics, and complex systems science. In
recent years, emergence has also become an important aspiration in robotics and interactive
systems, where designers seek to build collectives of simple agents that can adapt, reorganize,
and respond to changing environments.
As robotic systems increasingly operate in open-ended, uncertain, and human-centered
contexts, centralized control and exhaustive pre-programming become impractical. Swarm
robotics, modular robotics, and soft robotic systems promise scalability and adaptability,
yet they also raise a fundamental challenge: how to design systems whose behavior is not
explicitly scripted, but emerges through interaction. This thesis addresses this challenge by
treating emergence not merely as a phenomenon to be observed, but as a design problem to
be systematically explored.

1.1.1

Emergence as a Design Problem

Emergence is often celebrated for its aesthetic richness and adaptive potential, but it also
poses a diﬃculty for engineering and design. By deﬁnition, emergent behavior cannot be
fully predicted or controlled from the behavior of individual agents alone. While this indeterminacy is a strength in natural systems, it complicates the design of robotic collectives
intended to function reliably in real-world environments.
From a design perspective, the central question is not whether emergence can occur, but
under what conditions it arises, how its properties vary, and how it can be shaped without
eliminating its open-ended character. As robotic systems expand toward architectural scale,
adaptive materials, and long-term human interaction, understanding how emergent behavior

can be intentionally inﬂuenced becomes increasingly important. Designing for emergence
therefore represents a shift from prescribing outcomes to designing the conditions under
which behaviors unfold.

1.1.2

Limits of Current Swarm Robotics Approaches

Despite extensive research in swarm robotics and multi-agent systems, current approaches
tend to emphasize algorithmic control and optimization. Many systems rely on ﬁxed local
policies, centrally tuned parameters, or learning-based controllers that aim to produce speciﬁc target behaviors. While eﬀective in controlled settings, such approaches often struggle
to generalize, adapt, or scale when system morphology, environment, or human involvement
changes.
At the same time, advances in soft robotics and modular robotic systems highlight the
role of physical embodiment in shaping behavior. Material properties, geometry, actuation
mechanisms, and connectivity can constrain and enable interactions in ways that reduce
control complexity. However, embodiment is frequently treated as an engineering constraint
rather than as an explicit design variable for shaping collective behavior.
Human–robot interaction research further complicates this picture. While humans are
commonly involved as supervisors, operators, or collaborators, they are rarely considered as
participants within the feedback loops that govern emergent swarm behavior. As a result, the
interaction between organizational structure, embodied constraints, and human intervention
remains underexplored.
Taken together, these limitations point to a gap: although emergent behavior can be
produced in robotic swarms, there is limited understanding of how it can be systematically
shaped through design choices across organization, embodiment, and human interaction.

1.1.3

Research Questions and Hypotheses

As shown in Figure 1.1, emergent swarm behavior is shaped through a closed-loop interaction
between organizational policies, embodied constraints, and human feedback.
This thesis investigates how emergent behavior in shape-changing robot swarms can
be intentionally inﬂuenced through design. Rather than optimizing a single controller or
task performance metric, the research focuses on how design decisions alter the richness,
adaptability, and legibility of collective behavior.
The central research question is:
How can diﬀerentiated organization and local policies increase the complexity of
emergent behavior in shape-changing robot swarms?

H1 Organizational Diﬀerentiation Hypothesis. Introducing role diﬀerentiation

Together, these hypotheses frame emergence as an outcome shaped by organizational
structure and embodied design, rather than solely by control algorithms.

and human intervention. The research progresses from conceptual grounding, to formal
modeling, and ﬁnally to empirical evaluation across multiple experimental modalities.

Background and Literature Review

2.1

Dialectical Framing: From Opposition to Co-evolution

To situate this research, I begin not with a singular deﬁnition, but with a series of tensionspairs of concepts that appear contradictory yet are in fact mutually constitutive. Rather
than resolving these oppositions, this thesis treats them as productive dialectics: dynamic
relationships that generate new forms of thinking, designing, and computing.
Natural vs. Artiﬁcial
Everything originates from nature, yet human intelligence has introduced a peculiar rupture:
the capacity to produce artifacts. This marks the emergence of the artiﬁcialnot as something
outside nature, but as a recursive extension of it.
Antoni Gaudís claimstraight lines belong to men, curves to Godreveals a deeper distinction: artiﬁcial systems historically favor abstraction, regularity, and control, while natural
systems embody continuous variation, adaptation, and emergence.
However, this boundary is increasingly unstable. Contemporary bio-inspired design, synthetic biology, and soft robotics suggest that artiﬁcial systems are no longer merely imposed
upon nature but can grow, adapt, and behave like natural ones. The artiﬁcial, in this sense,
becomes a second-order naturea designed ecology of behaviors and materials.
Analog vs. Digital
Before digital computation, machines operated through continuous physical processesvoltages, rotations, and ﬂuid ﬂows. Analog systems compute through physical equivalence: the
system itself embodies the phenomenon it models.
The tide prediction machine is emblematic: instead of calculating tides symbolically, it
physically simulates them through gears and pulleys. Computation here is not abstractit is
material, embodied, and inseparable from physics.

Digital computation, by contrast, abstracts the world into discrete symbols (0/1), enabling scalability, precision, and programmability. Yet this abstraction comes at a cost: it
separates computation from materiality.
Today, we observe a return toward hybrid paradigms, where computation is redistributed
between digital control and physical form. This shift suggests that the analog/digital divide
is not binary but a spectrum of where computation happens: in code, in matter, or across
both.
Predictable vs. Unpredictable
Digital systems privilege predictability. Discrete states, deterministic logic, and formal rules
make behavior controllable and optimizable. This aligns with top-down design paradigms:
deﬁne rules, execute processes, and predict outcomes.
In contrast, natural and many analog systems exhibit unpredictabilitynot as noise, but as
emergence. Systems such as reaction-diﬀusion, slime mold growth, or ﬂocking behaviors generate complex global patterns from simple local interactions. These are bottom-up processes,
where outcomes cannot be fully prescribed in advance.
Importantly, unpredictability is not the opposite of computation; it represents a diﬀerent
mode of it. It reﬂects systems where computation unfolds through interaction rather than
instruction.

2.1.1

Synthesis: From Dialectics to Design Space

Across these three dialectics, a pattern emerges:
• Natural ↔ Artiﬁcial: from separation to co-evolution
• Analog ↔ Digital: from substitution to distribution of computation
• Predictable ↔ Unpredictable: from control to negotiation with emergence
These shifts collectively point toward a new paradigm: computation is no longer
conﬁned to symbolic processing, but extends into material, morphological, and
behavioral domains.
This aligns with the notion of morphological computation, where physical form ()
participates in information processing. Instead of treating the body as passive and control
as centralized, intelligence is distributed across structure, material, and environment. Such
an approach challenges the dominance of top-down control and foregrounds the co-evolution
of form and behavior.

Toward Reconﬁgurable Morphological Systems

Within this expanded computational paradigm, reconﬁgurable soft robotic systems
emerge as a particularly compelling site of investigation.
• From the natural–artiﬁcial dialectic, they inherit the capacity to behave like living
systemsadaptive, compliant, and environment-responsive.
• From the analog–digital dialectic, they embody hybrid computation: material deformation (analog) coupled with sensing, learning, and control (digital).
• From the predictable–unpredictable dialectic, they operate at the edge of controlwhere
behavior is not fully scripted but shaped through interaction and feedback.
In particular, inﬂatable modular robotics introduces an additional layer: reconﬁgurability through structure. Inﬂation enables reversible, lightweight, and continuous morphological change, while modularity allows discrete recombination. Together, they form a system
that navigates between continuity and discreteness, stability and transformation.
Such systems are not merely robotsthey are computational morphologies: machines
whose intelligence arises from the interplay between shape (form), matter (materiality),
and algorithm (control).

2.2

Emergent Behavior in Complex Systems

From local interaction to global organization. Emergent behavior is commonly described as coherent global organization arising from local interactions among many components, without a centralized controller. Classic accounts emphasize distributed feedback,
nonlinear interactions, and sensitivity to initial conditions as mechanisms through which
macroscopic patterns form and persist [2, 3]. In this view, self-organization is not an added
module of control, but an outcome of repeated local rules and coupling between agents and
environment.
Deﬁning “complex systems” and “emergence.” Despite broad usage, “complex system” and “emergence” remain contested terms. Ladyman et al. argue that many commonly
cited features are too vague to be analytically useful, and highlight the need to distinguish
between qualitative descriptions and operational measures [4]. Prokopenko et al. further
note that miscommunication often stems from unclear relations between complexity, selforganization, emergence, and adaptation, motivating complexity measures that can serve as

Chapter 2. Background and Literature Review

9

a shared vocabulary [5]. Complementing these eﬀorts, de Haan proposes a framework for
comparing emergence across domains by re-centering the role of the observer and abstracting
away from speciﬁc mechanisms [6].
Gap: controllability in designed systems. Much of the complex systems literature
either studies naturally occurring phenomena or evaluates emergence in abstract computational models. While these works clarify how emergence arises, they less frequently address
how emergence can be tuned in engineered artifacts: i.e., how designable parameters shape
the reachable space of emergent behaviors, and how to compare emergent outcomes across
diﬀerent embodiments, organizations, and interventions using shared evaluation criteria [4, 5].
This gap becomes critical when emergence is treated as a design target rather than merely
an observed phenomenon.

2.3

Emergence in Robotic Systems

Robotic swarms and modular robotic systems oﬀer an engineering context where local interaction rules, sensing/actuation constraints, and communication topology can be explicitly
designed. In these systems, emergence is not only a scientiﬁc object of explanation but
also a practical concern: emergent behaviors must be robust, interpretable, and controllable
enough to support task performance and human operation.

2.3.1

Soft Robotics and Morphological Computation

Embodiment as a generator of behavior. A central argument in embodied intelligence is that morphology and material properties can function as computational resources,
oﬄoading complexity from centralized control to body–environment dynamics. Pfeifer and
Bongard articulate how bodies shape perception-action loops and enable adaptive behavior
through morphology-dependent interactions [7]. Related design principles emphasize ecological balance and sensory-motor coordination, framing intelligence as an emergent property
of coupled morphology, materials, environment, and control [8].
Material-to-behavior mapping and reducing control complexity. In soft robotics,
compliance, damping, and distributed actuation can produce rich behaviors with comparatively simple control signals. For example, multifunctional ﬂuidic units can embody sensing,
actuation, and control within physical circuits, enabling self-synchronization and other passive emergent dynamics through mechanical coupling [9]. Such systems motivate a view of

Chapter 2. Background and Literature Review

10

“behavior” as partially embedded in material and geometric constraints, rather than purely
encoded in software.
Relevance to this thesis. For swarm robotics, embodiment inﬂuences the behavioral
repertoire available to local policies: what counts as a feasible interaction, what patterns are
physically stable, and how quickly a swarm can adapt. This thesis treats embodiment not
as a backdrop, but as a controllable layer that shapes emergent outcomes and interacts with
organizational structure and human intervention [7, 8].
Modular and Reconﬁgurable Robotic Systems
Topology, connectivity, and reconﬁguration. Modular robotic systems (MRS) formalize reconﬁguration as a core capability: agents can form and break connections, change
topology, and adapt morphology to tasks and environments. Ahmadzadeh and Masehian
provide a comprehensive synthesis of MRS methods spanning abstraction, planning, control,
and synchronization, while also highlighting persistent constraints in connection strength,
communication, and coordinated action [10]. Landmark systems such as M-Blocks demonstrate self-reconﬁguration through momentum-driven lattice movements and magnetic bonding, illustrating the tight coupling between mechanical design and the space of achievable
collective behaviors [11? ].
Large-scale collective organization and self-assembly. At swarm scale, programmable
self-assembly demonstrates how large numbers of simple robots can produce complex global
forms under local rules [12]. In parallel, work on programmable self-assembly in materials and colloidal systems frames “programmability” as the design of interaction rules and
building blocks that reliably yield target structures [13]. These precedents provide both inspiration and caution: emergence can be achieved, but systematic comparison across diﬀerent
organizational designs and embodied constraints remains challenging.
From ﬁxed rules to learned/evolved policies. Recent approaches use evolutionary
and learning-based methods to discover distributed policies that yield emergent behaviors,
shifting the focus from hand-designed rules to exploration of a behavior space. Rajbhandari and Sofge, for example, use evolutionary training to produce desired emergent group
behaviors across diﬀerent robot embodiments in simulation [14]. Related work explicitly
analyzes emergent behaviors under indirect swarm control, further emphasizing the need for
characterization methods beyond task success [15].

Gap: comparable studies of organization and feedback. Although prior work demonstrates that reconﬁguration, connectivity, and local policies can yield complex swarm behaviors, there remains a lack of systematic, comparable studies that isolate how organizational diﬀerentiation (e.g., role specialization, hierarchy) and feedback strategies (e.g., local
vs. broadcast communication) modulate emergent complexity, robustness, and adaptability
across embodiments and tasks [10, 14]. This thesis addresses this gap by proposing a comparative evaluation framework that links designable organizational factors to measurable
emergent outcomes.

2.3.2

Human Intervention and Cybernetic Control

Humans as part of the behavioral loop. In many real deployments, swarms are not
fully autonomous: human operators observe swarm state, interpret emergent patterns, and
intervene to steer collective behavior. Human-robot interaction research frames this as an
ongoing challenge, especially when robot behavior is complex, adaptive, or diﬃcult to explain
[16]. For swarm settings, human understanding is not merely an interface problem; it shapes
the feasibility of control and collaboration.
Perception and legibility of emergent behaviors. A key prerequisite for human inﬂuence is the ability to recognize and interpret emergent swarm behaviors under noise and
time constraints. Walker et al. study human recognition of canonical emergent behaviors
(rendezvous, ﬂocking, dispersion), showing diﬀerences in recognition diﬃculty and sensitivity
to viewing conditions [17]. These results motivate treating legibility and interpretability as
measurable success criteria rather than informal user impressions.
Human feedback as a driver of discovery. Beyond recognition, human feedback can be
integrated into the process of discovering and categorizing emergent behaviors. Mattson and
Brown propose a human-in-the-loop approach that learns a similarity space over collective
behaviors and combines novelty search with clustering to discover a richer taxonomy of
emergent behaviors in simulation [18]. This line of work reframes “human intervention” from
direct teleoperation to a cybernetic role: humans shape exploration objectives, preference
models, and the evaluation of novelty.
Gap: evaluation framework for how intervention changes complexity. Although
prior work studies perception, interaction, and human-guided discovery in swarms, there
is still no widely adopted framework that quantiﬁes how diﬀerent intervention modalities
change emergent behavior complexity over time—for example, whether intervention increases

behavioral diversity, improves robustness, or collapses complexity into more predictable patterns [17, 18]. This thesis targets this gap by linking intervention modalities to measurable
changes in emergent behavior metrics, and by positioning humans as an explicit element in
the feedback loop rather than an external operator.

Problem Framing and Models

3.0.1

Models as a Research Methodology

This thesis treats models not only as descriptive diagrams but as a research methodology for
structuring design, experimentation, and evaluation. Following the Design Research Methodology (DRM) proposed by Blessing and Chakrabarti [1], the work uses explicit models to
link designable inputs, mediated system dynamics, and measurable outcomes. The Reference
Model synthesizes prior literature into a structured design space for emergent swarm behavior, while the Impact Model translates this structure into concrete interventions, hypotheses,
and evaluation criteria. Together, these models guide experimental design, ensure comparability across conditions, and frame emergence as a designable and assessable outcome rather
than a purely descriptive phenomenon.
Reference Model: Structuring Emergence as a Design Problem
To synthesize the diverse strands of literature reviewed above, this thesis introduces
a design-oriented reference model that frames emergent swarm behavior as a structured
design problem rather than a purely descriptive phenomenon. Drawing from complex systems
theory, swarm robotics, and humanrobot interaction, the model provides a shared conceptual
structure for reasoning about how emergent behaviors arise, how they can be inﬂuenced
through design, and how their outcomes can be evaluated.
As illustrated in Figure 3.1, the reference model is structured along two complementary
dimensions.
Input, Inﬂuence, and Output. Along the vertical axis, the model distinguishes between
Input factors, Inﬂuence mechanisms, and Output criteria. Input factors correspond to
parameters that can be directly designed and speciﬁed prior to system deployment, such as
communication topology, degree of organizational diﬀerentiation, module morphology, and
connection conditions. Inﬂuence factors represent mediated system dynamics that emerge
during operation, including feedback propagation, adaptability, pattern transition frequency,
13

Chapter 3. Problem Framing and Models

14

and locomotion capability. Output criteria capture the observable qualities by which emergent behavior is evaluated, including behavioral complexity, robustness, and legibility.
This separation reﬂects a central insight from complex systems research: emergent behavior cannot be directly controlled, but the conditions under which it arises can be systematically shaped through design choices. By distinguishing designable inputs from mediated
dynamics and evaluative outcomes, the model clariﬁes what aspects of emergence are controllable, what must be inferred through observation, and what constitutes meaningful research
results.
Human Factor, Organization, and Embodiment. Along the horizontal axis, the
model situates swarm behavior within a coupled socio-technical system. The Human Factor captures how humans observe, interpret, and intervene in swarm behavior, including sensitivity to human input and intervention modalities. The Organization domain describes
how agents coordinate through local policies, communication structures, role assignments,
and swarm scale. The Embodiment domain represents the physical and material constraints of the robotic modules, including morphing capability, connective ability between
modules, and locomotion aﬀordances.
Across these domains, the model highlights several mediating mechanisms emphasized
in prior work. Feedback range and communication topology shape the speed and locality of
information propagation; organizational diﬀerentiation determines how many distinct local
rules coexist within the swarm; and embodied constraints delimit the action space available
to local policies. Emergent behavior arises from the interaction of these mechanisms rather
than from any single factor in isolation.
Overall, the reference model functions as a conceptual synthesis of the background literature, establishing a structured vocabulary for discussing emergence as a design outcome
that is shapedbut not centrally controlledby human, organizational, and embodied factors.
Impact Model: Interventions, Hypotheses, and Evaluation Logic
While the reference model structures the design space, the impact model translates this
structure into a causal research logic that links design interventions to measurable changes in
emergent swarm behavior. In contrast to studies that present emergence as an observed side
eﬀect of control policies or learning algorithms, the impact model treats emergent complexity
as an explicit outcome variable.

mediated mechanisms and evaluative criteria.

From emergence as description to emergence as outcome. Much of the swarm
robotics literature demonstrates emergent behavior through compelling examples, yet often
lacks a framework for comparing how diﬀerent design choices alter the complexity of those
behaviors. The impact model addresses this gap by framing emergent complexity as a
dependent variable that can be systematically compared across organizational and embodied
conﬁgurations.
Design interventions. Two primary intervention dimensions are derived from the reference model. The ﬁrst concerns organizational diﬀerentiation, including the introduction
of heterogeneous agent roles within an otherwise homogeneous swarm. This may involve
diﬀerences in sensitivity thresholds, actuation roles, or communication ranges, while maintaining a shared control architecture. The second concerns morphological modularity,
in which robot modules gain shape-changing capabilities (e.g., inﬂatable or morphing structures) and more permissive connection conditions, expanding the set of physically realizable
collective behaviors.
Rather than prescribing global behavior, these interventions act indirectly through mediated mechanisms such as feedback range, adaptability, and reconﬁguration dynamics.
Hypotheses. Based on this causal structure, the thesis advances the following hypotheses:
H1 Organizational Diﬀerentiation Hypothesis. Introducing role diﬀerentiation
within an otherwise homogeneous shape-changing swarm will produce a greater diversity of
emergent behavior patterns and increased adaptability compared to a fully identical swarm
operating under matched conditions.
H2 Morphological Modular Geometry Hypothesis. Enhancing the morphing
ability and connection aﬀordances of robot modules will lead to a measurable increase in
emergent behavioral complexity, enabling a broader range of collective formations and improved robustness to environmental constraints.
Evaluation logic. To assess these hypotheses, the impact model emphasizes comparative
evaluation across conﬁgurations rather than reliance on a single task-based metric. Emergent
behavior is evaluated using a combination of behavioral richness, adaptability, robustness,
and legibility, allowing diﬀerent organizational and embodied designs to be analyzed on equal
terms.
Together, the reference and impact models establish a coherent bridge between the background literature and the proposed methods, framing emergence as a designable, measurable,
and comparable phenomenon in shape-changing robotic swarms.

Methodology

4.1

Methods

This thesis investigates a design-oriented question: how do organizational diﬀerentiation and
local interaction policies, together with embodied modular morphing, increase the emergent
behavioral complexity of a shape-changing robot swarm? To answer this question, we construct a multi-stage experimental pipeline that couples
(i) physics-based swarm simulation,
(ii) mixed-reality human-in-the-loop interaction tests,
(iii) physical prototyping, with a uniﬁed evaluation layer that operationalizes “emergent
complexity” into measurable proxies.
Methodologically, the work treats emergence as a mediated outcome: designable inputs
(module morphology, connection conditions, organizational structure, and human intervention channels) do not directly specify a target global behavior; instead, they shape the
conditions under which global patterns appear, persist, and transition. This perspective is
aligned with cybernetic framings of human-in-the-loop systems [16], and motivates an experimental design that emphasizes comparative regimes and perturbation-response analysis
rather than single-demo success.
Overview of experimental logic. We implement a two-layer simulation stack: a physical
layer in Isaac Sim that provides credible embodied constraints (contact, friction, connection
forces), and an interaction layer in Unity that injects human intervention as an input channel
while preserving ﬁrst-person/immersive observation. We then translate key assumptions
into physical prototypes to verify which emergent phenomena survive material uncertainty.
Across all modalities, we evaluate outcomes using a shared set of metrics:
(1) behavior repertoire and regime diversity,
(2) transition dynamics and adaptability,
(3) robustness under noise/perturbation,
(4) human legibility and intervention eﬀectiveness.

4.1.1

Simulation Development (Isaac Sim + Unity/MR)

Platform choice as “two-layer simulation”
We deliberately separate physics ﬁdelity from interaction ﬁdelity. Isaac Sim is used as
the authoritative physics engine for multi-agent dynamics: rigid-body simulation, contact
resolution, frictional interactions, and constraint-based connections are handled in a reproducible environment, allowing controlled trials and parameter sweeps. This choice supports
modular robotics assumptions where the feasibility of motion and reconﬁguration strongly
depends on contact geometry and connection constraints [10? , 11].
Unity is used as the human-facing interaction and visualization layer. As shown in the 4.1
it provides ﬁrst-person/immersive observation, conﬁgurable user input mappings, and logging
of human actions and subjective judgments. Crucially, the Unity layer does not replace
physics; instead, it acts as a mediated control surface that sends high-level interventions
(parameter modulation, triggers, or spatial cues) into the Isaac simulation. This “two-layer”
design directly supports the thesis framing of humans as part of the behavioral loop [16]:
humans observe emergent states, intervene through constrained channels, and re-observe the
resulting transitions.

Chapter 4. Methodology

20

Module modeling via behavior primitives and connection rules
A recurring failure mode in swarm studies is that collective comparisons become unstable when individual agent capabilities are underspeciﬁed. To avoid this, we construct
two explicit capability libraries for a single module: (i) a shape-changing primitive library
describing what morphing actions exist and how they unfold over time, and (ii) a connection
rule library describing when modules attach/detach and what constraints result. This abstraction strategy mirrors how modular robotics literature separates action capabilities from
coordination algorithms [10] and how lattice-based systems formalize feasible reconﬁguration
moves [11].
Shape-changing primitives (morphing). Each module exposes a small set of parameterized morphing primitives such as inﬂate/deﬂate pulses, sustained expansion, and oscillatory actuation. Primitives are deﬁned by time-series parameters (rise time, frequency,
amplitude), and by their geometric and contact consequences (volume change, contact area
change, passability through gaps). This operationalizes the morphological computation intuition that “body” and “material” contribute to behavior generation and reduce the burden
of centralized control [7, 8].
Connection rules (attachment constraints). Connection is treated as a stateful constraint with measurable thresholds. We implement: (1) trigger conditions (distance threshold, orientation threshold, contact area threshold), (2) strength parameters (maximum tension/torque before failure), (3) detach logic (exceeding force thresholds or intentional release),
and (4) topology constraints (multi-point attachment, maximum degree, and whether reconﬁguration is allowed). This parameterization is motivated by the fact that modular systems
often hinge on the tight coupling between kinematic feasibility and connection reliability [10?
].
Deliverables of A2. The output of this stage is not a “nice demo” but a calibrated
capability speciﬁcation: (i) repeatable primitive trajectories (morph curves over time), (ii)
stable attach/detach boundaries (trigger regions in parameter space), and (iii) empirical
distributions of failure modes (false attachments, premature detachments). These results
directly seed the physical prototype design by providing initial parameter ranges for actuation
bandwidth and connection tolerances.

Coordination typology: scanning organization × local policy
The goal of coordination experiments is to make organizational diﬀerentiation explicit
and comparable, so that the Organizational Diﬀerentiation Hypothesis (H1) can be tested
against a homogeneous baseline. Rather than implementing a single canonical swarm algorithm, we deﬁne a policy family and evaluate how behavior regimes change across organization structures and communication topologies.
Minimal comparative set (core controls). We implement at least three conditions:
• Homogeneous baseline: all agents share identical sensing thresholds, morphing
bandwidth, and connection preferences.
• Diﬀerentiated roles: at least two roles are introduced (e.g., explorer/anchor/relay),
realized by diﬀerent sensitivity thresholds, actuation bandwidths, or communication
radii.
• Topology variation: local neighbor sensing versus broadcast-like information sharing
(or sparse local graph versus global shared state).
This design isolates the eﬀect of diﬀerentiation from the eﬀect of communication structure,
aligning with modular robotics perspectives that treat abstraction, coordination, and synchronization as distinct layers of the problem [10].
Parameter sweep and regime discovery. For each condition, we run parameter sweeps
over a bounded design space: morph amplitude/frequency, connection thresholds/strength,
neighborhood radius, and role proportion. Each run produces spatiotemporal trajectories:
agent poses, velocity ﬁelds, connection graph time-series, and morph state sequences. The
analysis goal is to discover regimes (recurrent collective patterns) and quantify how the
regime landscape changes when organizational diﬀerentiation or morphing capability is introduced.
Taxonomy building through behavior embeddings. To avoid subjective cherry-picking
of “interesting” behaviors, we represent each trial by an aggregated behavior descriptor (e.g.,
time-series statistics of graph connectivity, density, alignment, and morph state occupancy).
We then cluster trials to form a taxonomy of emergent patterns and compute “richness” as
the number and diversity of clusters. This taxonomy-based approach is consistent with the
idea that one should explore and categorize the space of possible swarm behaviors rather than
target a single engineered endpoint; it also parallels methods that combine novelty-driven
exploration with clustering and similarity spaces [18].

A4. Mixed-reality human-in-the-loop experiments
Human-in-the-loop evaluation is included for two reasons. First, swarm behaviors are
often used in contexts where a human operator must recognize the current collective mode
and decide whether and how to intervene. Second, “organic” complexity has an irreducible
perceptual component: behaviors that are mathematically complex may still be illegible or
unusable for human supervision. Therefore, we separate two MR study aims and design
tasks accordingly.
Aim 1: legibility and recognition of emergent modes. We adapt a recognition-style
protocol: participants view short time windows of swarm behavior and categorize the observed mode (or detect whether a transition occurred), with conﬁdence ratings and response
times. This directly follows the premise that diﬀerent emergent behaviors have diﬀerent
perceptual recognizability, and that recognition performance changes with noise and viewing
time [17]. In our context, the key independent variables are organizational diﬀerentiation
(homogeneous vs role-diﬀerentiated), morphing capability (enabled vs disabled), and visual
noise/occlusion levels introduced in the MR display.
Aim 2: intervention eﬀectiveness under mediated control. Participants intervene
through constrained channels (e.g., adjusting a small set of policy parameters, triggering
role switches, or injecting spatial cues). We log intervention frequency, type, and timing,
then measure downstream changes in regime transitions, complexity metrics, and recovery
dynamics. This design treats intervention as indirect control rather than direct command,
aligning with frameworks that analyze emergent behaviors under indirect swarm control [15].
The study does not assume participants know which behaviors are possible or desirable beforehand; instead, it emphasizes the co-adaptive process of discovery and preference, echoing
the notion that human feedback can guide exploration of novel emergent behaviors [18].

To investigate how embodiment shapes emergent behavior in shape-changing robot swarms,
this thesis explicitly parameterizes robot design as a set of controllable experimental variables.
Rather than treating the robotic platform as a ﬁxed carrier of control policies, embodiment
is modeled as an active contributor to system dynamics, consistent with perspectives from
morphological computation and soft robotics. The selected variables deﬁne the design space
of the modular inﬂatable units and are systematically explored across simulation and physical
prototyping.

4.1.3

Physical Prototyping (embodiment as evidence)

Simulation is necessary for regime discovery, but insuﬃcient as evidence of embodied emergence. Physical prototypes are therefore used to test whether key phenomena persist under
frictional uncertainty, actuation drift, leakage, and imperfect connections. Importantly, the
prototype stage is scoped for comparative validation rather than maximal scale: the goal is
to reproduce regime contrasts (homogeneous vs diﬀerentiated; morphing vs non-morphing)
in a controllable small-N setting.
Single-module validation: morphology–behavior mapping
We ﬁrst validate that the simulated morphing primitives correspond to physically
achievable actuation signatures. For each primitive, we measure shape change amplitude,

Chapter 4. Methodology

26

response time, repeatability across cycles, and actuation curves (e.g., pressure or energy
proxy where available). This stage supports H2 by grounding the claim that morphing capability is a meaningful design input rather than a purely simulated aﬀordance. If modules
exhibit coupling-induced phenomena such as spontaneous synchronization, we document
these as embodied dynamics rather than controller artifacts, consistent with observations
that mechanically coupled ﬂuidic units can yield emergent passive behaviors [9].
Connection validation: attach/detach boundaries and constraint eﬀects
Next, we validate connection conditions with two-module and small-chain tests. We
measure connection success rate, failure modes, detach thresholds, and how connectivity constrains motion degrees of freedom. We explicitly compare ﬁxed connections versus reconﬁgurable connect/disconnect conditions. This stage directly operationalizes the Embodimentdomain variables “connective ability” and “connection conditions” and tests whether the
mediated variables (e.g., locomotion capability and transition frequency) shift as predicted
by the Reference Model.
Small-swarm comparative experiments (N ≈ 5–20)
Finally, we run minimal comparative experiments with a small swarm where role diﬀerentiation is physically instantiated. Role diﬀerences may be implemented through sensing
thresholds, actuation bandwidth, or connection preferences (e.g., a role that tends to anchor
via stronger connections versus a role that explores via faster morphing cycles). The objective is not to replicate thousand-robot demonstrations, but to establish a controlled setting
in which organizational diﬀerentiation is visible, repeatable, and measurable.
This stage also supports a broader design framing: rather than pushing controllability
upward into centralized planning, the work explores how controllability can be distributed
into building-block-level properties and interaction rules, resonating with directions that
treat reconﬁgurable matter as a programmable substrate [19].

4.1.4

Evaluation: operationalizing “complexity” into measurable
success criteria

A central methodological challenge is that “organic complexity” is an abstract goal. We
therefore evaluate success through three complementary metric groups aligned with the Output layer of the models: (C1) complexity and repertoire richness, (C2) adaptability and
robustness, and (C3) human legibility and intervention eﬀects. The emphasis is on using
multiple partially independent measures so that conclusions do not hinge on a single proxy.

Chapter 4. Methodology

27

Emergent complexity and behavioral repertoire
Repertoire size and taxonomy richness. Using the clustering-based taxonomy from A3,
we compute the number of distinct behavior categories observed under controlled conditions,
and how evenly trials populate those categories. This directly matches the measurable proxy
“numbers of distinct behavior patterns.” We report cluster counts, long-tail occupancy (rarebut-recurrent modes), and between-cluster separability.
Transition dynamics as complexity-in-motion. We quantify how often and how predictably the swarm switches between modes: transition frequency, mean dwell time per
mode, and transition reversibility. These measures align with the Reference/Impact Model
variables “frequency of pattern changes” and “adaptability.” In addition to raw counts, we
compute transition matrices to characterize whether diﬀerentiation increases the variety of
reachable modes or merely increases instability.
Information-theoretic descriptors (supporting evidence). To strengthen claims beyond taxonomy counts, we compute information-theoretic measures on time-series features
such as connectivity graph statistics, density ﬁelds, velocity alignment, and morph-state
occupancy. This follows the premise that complexity, self-organization, and emergence can
be discussed with an information-theoretic “dictionary” that improves comparability across
systems [5]. These metrics are treated as supporting evidence rather than the sole deﬁnition
of success.
Adaptability and robustness under perturbation
Emergent systems are often valued not for a single pattern, but for their ability to
remain functional under uncertainty. We therefore evaluate:
• Perturbation recovery time: time to return to a stable mode after external disturbances (agent removal, connection failures, pushes, terrain changes).
• Performance under noise: degradation curves of complexity proxies under sensing
noise, latency, and message loss.
• Graceful degradation: how regime richness changes as a function of reduced agent
count or impaired role capabilities.
These tests also reﬂect practical constraints in modular robotic systems where resources,
communication, and connection strength limit real-world deployments [10].

Human factors: legibility, controllability, and intervention impact
Legibility and recognition accuracy. From the MR recognition tasks, we compute
recognition accuracy, response time, and conﬁdence across conditions. We further examine
how noise and viewing time aﬀect recognition, following the logic that recognition diﬀers by
behavior type and perceptual conditions [17]. This metric group connects directly to the
Output variable “legibility of robot behavior.”
Intervention eﬀectiveness as indirect control. For intervention trials, we quantify the
causal footprint of human input: intervention rate, input-to-response latency, and pre/post
changes in regime transitions and complexity proxies. Because the intervention channel is
mediated rather than direct, we interpret results in the context of indirect swarm control:
human inputs shift conditions, and global behavior responds through emergent dynamics
[15]. Where relevant, we analyze whether human input expands the behavioral repertoire
(discovering new modes) or compresses it (stabilizing a subset of modes).

4.1.5

Experimental design summary: hypotheses and interventions

The full experimental design is organized to test two primary hypotheses through explicit
interventions and controlled comparisons:
H1: Organizational Diﬀerentiation Hypothesis. Introducing role diﬀerentiation within
an otherwise homogeneous swarm will yield a larger and more diverse emergent repertoire
than the baseline. This is tested by comparing homogeneous versus diﬀerentiated-role conﬁgurations under matched embodiment primitives and matched environment settings, evaluating changes in taxonomy richness, transition structure, and robustness/legibility measures.
H2: Morphological Modularity Hypothesis. Introducing morphing ability and richer
connection conditions at the module level will increase measurable behavioral complexity.
This is tested by comparing morph-enabled versus morph-disabled modules (or restricted
primitives), while holding organizational policy families constant, and then validating key
contrasts in physical prototypes.
Interventions. Interventions occur at three levels: (1) Design-level interventions (morph
primitives, connection rules), (2) Organization-level interventions (role diﬀerentiation, topology), and (3) Human-level interventions (MR input channel and timing). The evaluation

framework is structured to detect not only improved complexity but also the trade-oﬀ space
among complexity, robustness, and legibility.
