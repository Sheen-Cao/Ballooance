# 1. Dialectic Debating of Related Concepts — Analysis of Contradictions in the Context of Computation

To start my research paper, I want to open a dialectic debate first in order to understand the key conceptual components related to the broader research scope. So I list out a few seemingly contradictory yet unified word pairs to frame the inquiry. This is the way I perceive my research at a higher level and seek to explore philosophical understandings in depth. It also might help with situating the research.

## Natural vs. Artificial

Everything is created in nature and by nature. However, as humans developed more and more intelligence, there was one moment when humans could create something by themselves that we call *artifacts*. That is the moment the artificial emerged and was divided from the natural. But what do the artifacts look like? *"Straight lines belong to men, curves to God."* This famous claim made by the architect Antoni Gaudí discloses a quiet asymmetry: that the things we make tend to be reductive, gridded, and orthogonal, while the things that grow tend to be redundant, curved, and tolerant of variation. For most of industrial history this asymmetry was not a problem — it was the point. Standardization, repeatability, and right angles were how we got cheap parts, predictable assemblies, and machines that could be reasoned about.

Yet the boundary has been quietly dissolving for some time. Biomimicry, generative design, and material-driven fabrication have all pulled artifacts back toward curves — toward shapes that are grown rather than drawn. A Voronoi-tessellated bone scaffold or a 3D-printed lattice optimized through topology optimization is not really straight or curved in Gaudí's sense; it is the shape that *arises* from a set of constraints, much like a leaf or a coral. In robotics, the same drift can be felt: rigid links and revolute joints, the Cartesian skeleton of the twentieth-century machine, are increasingly accompanied — sometimes replaced — by elastomers, fluids, and pneumatically pressurized membranes that behave less like mechanisms and more like organisms. The natural and the artificial are, in this sense, no longer two countries with a hard border. They are two ends of a gradient, and many of the most interesting design spaces today live somewhere in the middle.

## Physical vs. Digital

Before the digital computer was designed, people mostly relied on analog signals processed by physical machines — radios, phonographs, telephones, mechanical calculators. Analog signals come from the variation of physical quantities, such as voltage, pressure, or position. A particularly elegant example is the *tide-predicting machine* developed by William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) in 1872: a series of pulleys, gears, and weighted strings was engineered to mechanically sum the harmonic constituents of tidal motion. The machine did not represent the tide as numbers; it *was* the tide, in miniature. Computation here was inseparable from material — the gears were the equations, and the rope tension carried the answer.

The digital turn replaced this kind of computation with discrete symbols. Voltage no longer *was* the value; it *encoded* the value. The shift was enormously powerful — programs could be copied, debugged, and shared in ways that a pulley assembly could not — but it also installed a particular metaphor at the center of how we think about machines: that intelligent behavior must come from a controller running symbols, and that the body is just the thing the controller commands. The quiet cost of this metaphor is that we tend to forget how much of a system's behavior was already being done by its physics. Recent work on physical computing — soft robots whose grasps are determined by passive material compliance rather than feedback loops, reservoir computers built from liquid surfaces or compliant bodies, and the broader program of *morphological computation* (Pfeifer & Bongard, 2007) — is, in part, an effort to reclaim what the digital metaphor abstracted away.

## Predictable vs. Unpredictable

Digital signals are usually treated as discrete and explicit: a 1 or a 0, a state that is either *on* or *off*. These normative digits are easier to manipulate, to verify, and to reason about, and so they have become the fundamental elements of computation as we typically practice it. Predictability is comfortable. It allows us to write proofs, to run unit tests, to specify guarantees.

Physical systems, by contrast, tend toward the unpredictable — not in the sense of being random, but in the sense of being *underdetermined* by any model we are willing to write down. A balloon does not inflate to exactly the same shape twice. Friction varies with humidity. Two nominally identical components fabricated on the same printer behave differently when actuated. In classical control engineering, this unpredictability is treated as noise — something to be modeled and rejected. But there is a different, much older tradition that treats it as a resource. Turing's reaction–diffusion patterns, the foraging trails of *Physarum polycephalum*, ant colony optimization, even Karl Sims's evolved virtual creatures — all of these are computations whose interesting behavior depends on a substrate that is not fully predictable, and whose outputs are emergent properties of that substrate rather than explicit instructions written into it. The unpredictability is what allows the system to *find* something rather than merely *execute* something. For a physical robot whose body is made of soft, pressurized material, this distinction stops being philosophical and becomes very practical: the parts of its behavior we cannot model in advance may be exactly the parts doing the most work.

## Top-down vs. Bottom-up

If predictability is the epistemic axis, top-down vs. bottom-up is the design axis. A top-down system specifies the global behavior first and decomposes it into commands for each component: a gait controller decides the robot's trajectory, then computes joint angles, then sends torques. A bottom-up system specifies only the *local* rules — what each component should do given what it can sense — and lets the global behavior arise from their interaction. Cellular automata, flocking models, and most of the swarm intelligence literature live on this side; so do reaction–diffusion morphogenesis and the self-assembly of programmable matter (Cademartiri & Bishop, 2015; Rubenstein et al., 2014).

Neither approach is universally better. Top-down systems are tractable, verifiable, and easy to debug; they shine when the task is well-specified and the world is cooperative. Bottom-up systems are robust, scalable, and degrade gracefully; they shine when the task is exploratory, the world is noisy, and the global behavior is too complex to specify directly. The interesting cases — and, I will argue, the case of an inflatable modular robot whose modules are physically coupled through their material — are the ones where neither approach alone is sufficient. The body is too compliant for a top-down controller to fully command, and the task is too goal-directed for a purely bottom-up rule set to satisfy. Some negotiation between the two is required.

## Soft vs. Rigid

Soft and rigid is, in some sense, the physical instantiation of the dialectics above. Rigid bodies are predictable in shape, well-modeled by classical mechanics, and amenable to top-down control — which is why nearly all of industrial robotics is built on them. Soft bodies trade that predictability for compliance: they conform to objects they touch, absorb shocks, redistribute load passively, and continuously change their effective shape under load. Importantly, this compliance is not just a property to be tolerated; it can be a property to be *recruited*. A soft gripper may grasp an irregular object using nothing but its own deformation, with no closed-loop feedback at all. The morphology, in this case, is doing the computation that a controller would otherwise have to do. This is the central insight of the soft robotics and morphological computation literatures — and it is the insight that makes the soft/rigid distinction more than a material choice. It is a choice about *where the intelligence lives*.

## Virtual vs. Actual

A final pair worth naming: the virtual and the actual. Simulation has become the default medium of robotics research, especially with the rise of differentiable physics and large-scale reinforcement learning. The virtual is fast, cheap, parallelizable, and forgiving. The actual is none of these things — but the actual is also where every claim is ultimately tested. The persistent gap between the two, the so-called *sim-to-real gap*, is usually framed as a problem to be closed: the better our simulators, the closer the virtual approaches the actual. But for soft, materially coupled systems, this framing can be misleading. The gap is not just measurement error; it is, in part, a measurement of how much of the robot's behavior was being produced by the body itself — the very behavior the rigid-body simulator could not represent. Treated this way, the sim-to-real gap is not a failure to be apologized for. It is evidence, and a methodological opportunity.

## Where these pairs converge

These dialectics are not independent axes — they cluster. The natural, the physical, the unpredictable, the bottom-up, the soft, and the actual all share a common posture: they treat behavior as something that *arises* from a substrate rather than something *imposed* on it. The artificial, the digital, the predictable, the top-down, the rigid, and the virtual share the opposite posture: behavior is specified, encoded, and executed. Most of contemporary robotics has been built on the second cluster — and has been extraordinarily successful within its domain. But a growing body of work, from soft robotics (Rus & Tolley, 2015) to morphological computation (Pfeifer & Iida, 2005), from collective and modular self-reconfigurable systems (Yim et al., 2007; Romanishin et al., 2013) to programmable matter and particle robots (Li et al., 2019), suggests that the next class of capabilities — adaptive, embodied, energetically efficient, robust to the messiness of the real world — will require us to take the first cluster seriously again, not as a return to pre-industrial craft but as a deliberate design strategy.

A *reconfigurable inflatable modular robot* sits squarely at the intersection of these clusters, which is why it is interesting. It is artificial, but its behavior is shaped by physical phenomena — air pressure, membrane elasticity, contact between neighboring inflated bodies — that resist neat symbolic description. It is built from discrete modules, but its motion is continuous and underdetermined. It is amenable to top-down command at the level of which valves to open, but its actual locomotion emerges from the bottom-up interaction of inflated neighbors and gravity. It is soft in its surface and rigid in its topology. It can be simulated, but only partially — the sim-to-real gap is precisely where the material is doing its work. In other words, almost every dialectic above passes through this object. That is what this thesis takes as its starting condition. The chapters that follow ask, in different ways, what it means to design — and to control — a robot whose body is not a passive container for computation but an active participant in it.
